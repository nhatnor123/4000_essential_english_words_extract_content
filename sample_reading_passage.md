The Secret Under the Northern Castle

Maya felt anxious on the morning she began her journey. The doctor had told her that one lung was still weak after the flu, so she should move with gentle motion and keep a slow pace. Maya tried to be polite to everyone she met, but she did not possess much confidence yet. When her little brother ran rapidly into the kitchen and made a careless remark about her pale face, she tried not to react. She only went to seek a towel because he had knocked over a cup and caused tea to spill across the table. Outside, the sun began to shine, and Maya decided that fear would not command her day.

Her uncle asked her to bring an old map to a remote castle near the northern border of the country. The map showed a southern road, a hidden temple, and a statue with one broken wing. Long ago, people believed a meteor had struck that land and created an explosion so large that steam rose from the ground for weeks. Some said a monster guarded the place, while others said it was only a machine from the sky. The castle had an upper tower, a garden full of weed, and a room where ancient jewelry had been stored. Before she left, her uncle gave her counsel and said, “Submit your report only when you can ensure every detail is true.”

At the entrance of the castle, Maya met Chief Rowan, a brave old guard who carried a bow, an arrow, and a short weapon at his side. He had survived a military battle years before, so he knew that every enemy had a disadvantage if you could stay calm. “I intend to help you,” he said, “but you must obey my instructions unless you want trouble.” He picked up a log from the road and used it to secure the gate. Maya could hardly trust a stranger at first, but his steady voice and warm laughter made her relax. Then he told her to twist the iron handle slowly because the door was old.

Inside the narrow hallway, Maya felt her chest tighten again. The rough walls seemed to disturb every breath she took, and she had to estimate how far she could walk before resting in the shade. Rowan tried to impress her with stories of honor and courage, but she was too sensitive to sounds; even a mouse could make her scream. She thought the journey might become a disaster, and the possible consequence and threat frightened her. Still, she wanted to satisfy her curiosity. She knew that a marathon was completed one step at a time, so she took a supplement, faced her terror, and refused to become a helpless victim of fear.

They found a carving of Maya’s ancestor above a stone door. The figure wore a thick boot, held a pure jewel, and stood at a strange angle beside the border of a painted world. Rowan wanted to congratulate Maya because the discovery matched an incredible legend told by her senior relative. The legend said that heaven sent a silent star to sink into the earth and wrap itself in stone. The frame around the carving was superior work, and praise for the artist seemed natural. But before they could proceed, shadows began to surround them, and Maya noticed that the floor was not stable.

A young guy named Elias appeared from a side passage. He had studied abroad but returned to help his tribe protect the palace. He spoke in a brief but lovely way and asked for a small fee before he would permit them to enter the royal chamber. Rowan felt anger and wanted to protest, but Maya offered a favor instead: she would exchange a silver coin for information. Elias explained that his sister, a bride in the nearby village, had begged him to chase away thieves who tried to dive into the underground stream. His mood was serious as he pointed to a broken sculpture near the gate. He did not want to disappoint his family, and he hoped the palace would remain safe forever, especially for the youth of the valley.

Elias explained the basis of their mission. His colleague, a scholar of biology and ancient machines, had discovered a metal cage near the ruins of an old colony. The discovery caused debate among researchers because some believed the cage was factual proof of an overseas visitor, while others said it was only a tool from a volcano mine. The idea did not depress Maya; it began to fascinate her. She had no occupation as a scientist, nevertheless she was willing to learn. Their route would depart from the castle, cross a ravine, and lead to a significant site where they might persuade the village council to protect the area.

The path through the broad valley was difficult. A thorny bush scratched Maya’s arm, but she felt capable of continuing. Elias warned that some guides cheat travelers and dictate false directions, so Maya tried to concentrate on the map. She could not conclude their definite destination yet, but she was confident they were close, and that thought filled her with delight. At the edge of a cliff, a shadow moved behind them. The sound was considerable, and Rowan tried to convey a silent warning with his hand. They reached a small resort once used by miners, but Maya began to suspect that someone else wanted them to succeed only so he could steal what they found.

In the village capital, many people came to admire the team, but the authority there was divided. Some wanted to aid the attempt to defend the ruins from destruction; others feared disorder. A royal official said cooperation would enable them to govern the site better and relieve poverty by bringing tourists and increasing local income. However, a rich merchant with a bad reputation wanted plenty of control. “Do not cooperate with outsiders,” he warned. “They will frustrate our plans, create division, and make us look stupid.” Maya saw that the people had to struggle not like slave workers, but like citizens deciding their own future.

That evening, every citizen gathered before the council. The elder stood in the fog and prepared to declare that the project was either an enormous opportunity or an extraordinary danger. A funeral bell rang from a nearby temple, giving everyone a strange impression. An angry man became mad and said they ought to resist Maya’s team. He claimed the ruins would reveal a violent curse, a giant trap, and a sword from an old tale. Rowan tried to rid the crowd of fear by explaining that no trial could begin without evidence. Maya listened carefully, because she knew fear could become a monster when no one questioned it.

The next morning, the village principal gave Maya admission to a small school exhibit about astronomy, chemistry, geography, and even a dinosaur bone found near the stream. Despite the wet floor, one row of students stood lightly beside each shelf. A gentle boy with a super memory recited the weather forecast and explained how volcanic dust could interfere with sunlight. In spite of the excitement, some parents still tried to blame Maya for bringing danger. The principal, a quiet genius with local fame, asked everyone not to attack a visitor before they had heard the facts.

At lunch, Elias showed Maya the community kitchen. No one there would abuse a guest, even if they could not afford much food. A generous baker used a candle to warm a small oven and bake bread with bean paste. “The main ingredient is patience,” she said. The village had a debt problem, so people used a fund to buy grain and metal tools. They had to monitor the quantity of supplies and insist that everyone share fairly and decrease waste. Some villagers wanted to convert the kitchen into a tourist café, but others oppose the idea. “If a company creates a mess or finds fault with us, they might sue,” one passive man said.

During an emergency meeting about a storm that could cost the village a billion coins in damage, the team prepared to hike toward the peak. Maya felt new anxiety, but she refused to quit. Rowan said an army could not climb faster than the slowest person, and he offered to lend her his walking stick. The initial climb was intense, and even a tiny insect could make her spin when it landed on her neck. Still, she had potential. Elias promised to consult a mountain guide and guarantee a safe trail. Maya felt pride when she reached the top, where a fortune of carved stones waited. A tutor had once taught her how to carve symbols, and now she recognized proof that the stones formed a map.

From the peak, it became apparent and obvious that the old builders were not blind to science. They knew how to calculate the sun’s path, and their drawings showed an understanding of physics. Maya wanted to chat about the discovery all night, but she had to commit herself to careful notes. In the dormitory, she tried to compose a thesis about the site, though the cold air began to exhaust her. A secretary from the council arrived in a uniform to remind her that severe storms were coming. “Ignore the gossip,” the secretary said. “Your talent and vision may save this greenhouse valley, but each portion of your report must be clear.”

The next day, Maya met her boss from the museum and a committee of researchers. They asked her to sign a contract and join their crew. Her task was to devote herself to careful digging, but not to dig so deep that she damaged the foundation of the ruins. At sunset, everyone gathered to dine on soup with a unique flavor from mountain herbs. A farmer offered to donate vegetables, and another promised to double the supply if the work helped the next generation. Maya learned how each layer of mud, soil, and smooth stone could absorb water, elevate a structure, and handle the weight of history.

Behind a sealed wall, they discovered a circular chamber. A nervous worker tried to deny that he had touched the door, but a document showed someone had opened it before. Maya wanted to emphasize safety, so she told everyone to freeze when the floor began to shake. A miner with a fever and flu made a weak gesture, trying to interrupt the search, but Rowan told him the last decision belonged to Maya. Inside, a statue had the likeness of an astronaut. Moreover, the discovery changed everyone’s perspective. A rational person could recover from shock, rely on evidence, and still stare in wonder. Thus, even shy Maya began to speak with strength.

The team’s aim was to attach a signal rope to an old carriage and lower supplies into the cave. A classic mistake, Rowan warned, was to bet that ancient structures were always safe. Elias had to commute from the village each morning, so Maya asked him to confirm every expense in a formal record. A junior mechanic helped repair the wheels and measure the height of the tunnel. During the prime shift, some workers began to criticize the plan because their ideas differ from Maya’s. She gave a sincere answer: “We must respect labor, but we must also invent better methods when danger appears.”

The village had great ability in agriculture, but the discovery made everyone curious about a different profession. Children drew a cartoon of the astronaut on the ceiling of the school and wrote a diary about each delay in the search. The project became an element of local faith. A monk and a priest came to greet the team, bringing grain and quiet joy. They asked Maya to investigate the chamber but to label every object with care. One odd symbol made her pause. It seemed to convince her that the builders had mixed religion, science, and art in a way no one had expected.

Later, the council voted to adopt a rule: no one could enter the chamber without permission, and no speech could extend beyond the planned reception. Some treasure hunters would beg to go beyond the barrier and try to fool the guards, but guards would forbid them. During the ceremony, actors wore costume and tried to illustrate the origin of the legend. “Indeed!” one child would exclaim whenever the hero crossed the stream. Maya had to interpret the ancient text kindly, because the old tone was not always clear. She believed the motive of the builders was not greed but protection. Above the entrance, a bird had made a nest, and its silence seemed to reject the noise of the crowd.

Maya promised to accomplish one final task before leaving. She needed the council to approve an approximate map of the underground rooms. A stone barrier made it difficult to detect every passage, but she felt it was her duty to try, not just pretend that the work was finished. The plan was elementary at first, and failure seemed possible. Over several days, progress was gradual. An immigrant miner helped insert markers into the floor, while a retired teacher tried to create a statistic about visitor safety. The team needed instant decisions about where to refrigerate medicine, where to rent equipment, and when each worker could retire from a dangerous shift. Soon, Maya earned recognition for her rank as lead researcher.

On the final night, Maya stayed awake beside the underground telescope. The ancient device seemed able to follow a satellite, study gravity, and measure the scale of the sky. A miner explained that each mineral around it was raw but beautiful, and one jewel near the lens seemed to float in the air. The council had given a grant for students to participate in future research, and Maya had received permission to pour clear oil into the machine. In the presence of the villagers, she had the courage to stretch her hand, skip her fear, and turn the final wheel. The telescope opened, and deep under the earth, the stars appeared.
